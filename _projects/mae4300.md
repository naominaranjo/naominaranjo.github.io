The Boeing 737 MAX accidents are often described as a technical failure, but in my Week 6–9 work the case reads more like an ethical failure that spread across design, communication, and oversight. The key issue is not only that MCAS existed, but that the system’s scope and failure behavior were not treated as safety-critical information for all stakeholders. I initially framed the case around questions of disclosure and responsibility, like whether Boeing should have been more transparent about MCAS and whether pilots should have been given clearer training materials. Those early questions grew into a broader picture: safety depends on what people know, what they are encouraged to report, and how institutions handle risk. In that sense, the MAX case shows how “small” choices become catastrophic when they align in the wrong direction.

A central ethical problem is transparency, especially toward the people who must manage risk in real time. My Week 7 notes emphasize that MCAS could repeatedly push the aircraft nose-down through stabilizer movement, creating an escalating situation for pilots and passengers.When a safety-critical system can behave that aggressively, transparency cannot just mean meeting a minimum disclosure standard. It must mean giving pilots a reliable understanding of what the aircraft might do and how to respond under pressure. This connects directly to my Week 6 concern about whether Boeing minimized MCAS in training manuals and why that matters ethically. If pilots are denied clarity, then the system shifts risk onto them without giving them the tools to control it. 

The accountability problem also shows up in the regulatory structure and the possibility of conflicts of interest. In Week 6, I raised whether companies should effectively self-regulate through FAA delegation, because that structure can blur who is responsible for catching safety risks. In Week 7, I noted that federal law allowed the FAA to delegate certain certification functions, which shapes how oversight actually occurs in practice. Even if individuals intend to do the right thing, a system that depends on the regulated organization to validate itself can weaken independent skepticism. In practice, that can make “passing certification” feel like the goal, even when the ethical goal should be protecting the public. This is why the case feels systemic rather than simply a single bad engineering decision. 

Economic pressure is the other force that kept reappearing in my analysis. In Week 6 I asked whether economic interests drive preventable crashes, because the incentives behind decisions determine what gets prioritized. In Week 7 organizational facts, I included the idea that financial and competitive pressures influenced training expectations and program decisions, which can indirectly shape safety outcomes. In a safety-critical industry, cost and schedule constraints are real, but they cannot ethically override decisions that affect life-and-death risk. When the organization treats safety as something that can be argued down rather than built in with margin, the public becomes the one absorbing uncertainty. The MAX case demonstrates that “business decisions” can still be engineering ethics decisions when they affect safety-critical communication, testing, and redundancy.

Finally, Week 9 helped explain why the ideal ethical response is not always what happens in real organizations. I identified practical constraints like organizational pressure, regulatory ambiguity, limited individual influence, and cultural norms that discourage challenging the momentum of a major program. Those constraints do not excuse outcomes, but they help explain how failures become normalized until something breaks publicly. The lesson I take from Weeks 6–9 is that safety must be treated as a duty, not a compliance outcome, and that transparency must be judged by whether it protects real people in real failure conditions. Preventing similar failures requires action at the individual level, organizational level, and system level, not just a better technical fix after the fact.
